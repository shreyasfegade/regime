    How an HMM sees the market

    A hidden Markov model never sees "bull" or "bear". It sees numbers, one day at a time, and guesses which of a few hidden states produced them. Here is that process, step by step, on ten years of the NIFTY 50.

    1

    One line, ten years

    This is the NIFTY 50 from a decade ago to today: about 2,500 trading sessions. Read as a single line it says "up, with interruptions".

    The interruptions are the interesting part. A single average return or a single volatility figure for the whole decade describes no actual year in it.

    2

    Calm and storm take turns

    Underneath the price is its volatility: how large the daily moves were over the previous 20 sessions, scaled to a year. The ten-year average is the dashed line, but the series spends very little time near it. It ranges from quiet to extreme.

    Markets cluster: calm days follow calm days, wild days follow wild days. That persistence is what a regime model tries to capture.

    3

    What the model actually sees

    REGIME gives the model five numbers per day: the log return, volatility over 5 and 20 sessions, a volume z-score (how unusual that day's volume was), and 10-day momentum. Each is standardised so no feature dominates by its units.

    The model is never told what any day "was". It only sees these points and has to explain them.

    4

    Four hidden states, each a cloud of days

    The model assumes every day was produced by one of four hidden states, and that each state has its own five-dimensional Gaussian: a centre and a shape. Fitting the model (the Baum–Welch algorithm) moves those centres and shapes until the observed days are as likely as possible.

    The states arrive unnamed. REGIME names them afterwards from their centres: the highest-volatility state is Crisis, the quietest is Accumulation, and the other two split into Bullish and Bearish by their average return. On the NIFTY 50, Crisis covers a small share of all sessions.

    5

    States are sticky

    The "Markov" part is a transition matrix: the chance of moving from each state to each other state on the next session. Fitted to the NIFTY 50, a Bullish day is followed by another Bullish day most of the time, which implies spells of about several dozen sessions. A Crisis day is followed by another most of the time too, about a few dozen sessions on average.

    That stickiness is what turns a noisy day-by-day guess into regimes that last weeks. It is also memoryless: the expected time left in a state does not depend on how long it has already lasted.

    6

    Filtering: updating a belief, one day at a time

    Given the fitted model, the forward algorithm walks through the days in order. Each morning it carries yesterday's belief forward through the transition matrix; each evening it weighs that against how well each state explains the day just seen. The result is a probability for each state, using only data up to that day.

    That is exactly what is running in your browser now. With the fitted model, the most likely state changes a number of times in ten years, about ten a year.

    7

    Hindsight is cleaner than the live call

    Once all ten years are in, a second algorithm (Viterbi) finds the single most likely sequence of states for the whole history at once. It can use tomorrow's data to relabel today, so its path is smoother: fewer changes in total. The live, filtered call agrees with it on most of days.

    REGIME shows both on every stock page, and keeps them apart. The timeline bands and calendar use hindsight, because they describe the past. The backtest uses neither: it uses walk-forward models that were refitted only on data available at the time.

    What this is not

    It is not a forecast. The model describes the state the market appears to be in. The transition matrix gives probabilities for tomorrow, but they are averages over the past decade, not a view on the next session.

    The labels are a heuristic. The model finds four clouds; the names come from a simple rule on their centres. On some stocks, two states are close and the names matter less than the numbers. The robustness checks on every stock page say how stable the split is.

    Four is a choice. An information criterion (BIC) often prefers five or six states, partly because the features overlap in time. REGIME keeps four everywhere so a regime means the same thing on every page, and says so where BIC disagrees.

    In-sample pictures flatter. Anything decoded with hindsight looks cleaner than what was knowable at the time. That is why the backtest is walk-forward and pays realistic costs, and why its result is often worse than buy-and-hold.
